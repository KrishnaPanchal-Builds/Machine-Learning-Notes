Part A
Two philosophies of classification
1

Why train and test must agree

Learning is only possible because the future is assumed to resemble the past. Making that assumption precise is the first move of the lecture.

We fit a model on a training set and then judge it on a test set it has never seen. Two degenerate cases mark out the range of what can happen:

  • If the test data were simply a subset of the training data, any model that memorises would look perfect — and would have told us nothing.
  • If the test data were unrelated to the training data, nothing learnt could transfer, and the test error would be no better than guessing.

Useful learning lives between these extremes, and the bridge is a probabilistic assumption: there exists a fixed distribution \(\mathcal{D}\) over feature–label pairs, and every point we ever see — training or test — is an independent draw from it.

$$\mathcal{D}\ \text{is a distribution over}\ \ \textcolor{#1F8A85}{\mathcal{X}}\times\textcolor{#B23A6B}{\mathcal{Y}}, \qquad (x_i,y_i)\ \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\ \mathcal{D}$$
\(\mathcal{X}\) — FEATURE SPACEWhatever a data point is described by: a vector of word indicators, an age and a personality score, an image.
\(\mathcal{Y}\) — LABEL SET\(\{+1,-1\}\) or, \(\{0,1\}\).

A blackbox emits pairs \((x_i,y_i)\) drawn from \(\mathcal{D}\) — the training set is \(n\) such draws, and the test point is one more, from the same \(\mathcal{D}\).

When the assumption fails

Real deployments break it constantly. A clickbait detector trained on 2015 headlines meets 2026 headlines whose vocabulary has moved (covariate shift: \(P(x)\) changes). A spam filter meets an adversary who rewrites messages precisely to defeat it (adversarial shift). A medical model trained in one hospital meets another hospital's patient mix (\(P(y)\) changes — prior shift). None of the mathematics below is wrong in those settings; it is simply answering a question about a distribution that is no longer the one generating the test data.

2

Two ways to build a classifier

Every classifier ultimately needs \(P(Y\mid X)\), or at least an ordering of the classes. The two families differ in whether they aim at it directly or reach it through a detour.

Predict whether someone will attend a party \((Y)\) based on their age and personality \((X)\).

GENERATIVE — models \(P(X,Y)\)

$$P(X,Y)=P(X\mid Y)\,P(Y)$$

  • Asks how the world produces both \(X\) and \(Y\).
  • \(P(X\mid Y)\) — what ages and personalities are typical among attendees, and among non-attendees, separately.
  • \(P(Y)\) — how often people attend at all.
  • Gets \(P(Y\mid X)\) afterwards, by Bayes' rule.
  • Examples: naive Bayes, Gaussian discriminant analysis (LDA/QDA), Gaussian mixture models, hidden Markov models, and — in the modern sense — VAEs, diffusion models and autoregressive language models.
DISCRIMINATIVE — models \(P(Y\mid X)\)

$$P(Y\mid X)\ \text{directly}$$

  • Does not care how the data was generated.
  • Never asks what a typical attendee looks like; only where attendees stop and non-attendees start.
  • All modelling capacity is spent on the boundary.
  • Examples: logistic regression, neural networks, \(k\)-NN, decision trees, SVM, random forests, gradient boosting.
Sub-topic — a three-way taxonomy is cleaner than two

Bishop's standard division has three boxes, not two, and it dissolves several arguments about where \(k\)-NN belongs:

  1. Generative models — learn \(P(X\mid Y)\) and \(P(Y)\), obtain \(P(Y\mid X)\) by Bayes' rule. Can sample new data, detect outliers via \(P(x)\), handle missing features by marginalising.
  2. Probabilistic discriminative models — learn \(P(Y\mid X)\) directly, with calibrated probabilities. Logistic regression, softmax neural networks.
  3. Discriminant functions — learn a map \(x\mapsto\) label with no probability anywhere. Perceptron, hard-margin SVM, a decision tree read as a rule list.

A word about "generative AI"

The word generative in this lecture means "models the joint distribution, and so could in principle emit new samples". That is the same word used for image and text generators, and the sense is genuinely the same — an autoregressive language model is a generative model of \(P(\text{text})\). What differs is only ambition: naive Bayes could technically generate a headline by flipping 50,000 coins, and the result would be word salad. Being generative is a property of the factorisation, not a promise of quality.

Discriminative models, written as rules

Four familiar classifiers, each expressed as a statement about \(P(Y=1\mid X)\).

Each model answers "is \(P(Y=1\mid X)\) high enough?" in its own vocabulary — the majority label among the \(k\) nearest neighbours, the label stored at a leaf, the sign of a linear score, or a sigmoid crossing \(0.5\).

ModelRule as writtenWhat is really being modelled

Part B
Building a generative model for clickbait
4

Clickbait, as a bag of words

Before any probability can be written down, a headline has to become a vector.

The task: given a headline, decide whether it is clickbait \((y=1)\) or a normal headline \((y=0)\). The training set is

$$\{(X_1,Y_1),(X_2,Y_2),\dots,(X_n,Y_n)\},\qquad X_i\in\{0,1\}^{d},\quad Y_i\in\{0,1\}.$$

Each headline is represented by a binary bag of words.

Fix a vocabulary of \(d\) words, sorted; the \(j\)-th coordinate of \(X_i\) is \(1\) if word \(j\) occurs anywhere in headline \(i\) and \(0\) otherwise.

For a realistic vocabulary \(d\approx 50{,}000\).
Sub-topic — what this representation throws away

Three things, all deliberately:

  • Word order. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" get identical vectors. Hence bag of words.
  • Counts. A word appearing five times looks the same as once. That is a property of the binary bag of words; the multinomial variant in block 9 keeps counts.
  • Meaning. "film" and "movie" are two unrelated coordinates. Embeddings exist precisely to repair this.

The vector is also extremely sparse: a ten-word headline switches on ten coordinates out of 50,000, so \(99.98\%\) of every \(X_i\) is zero. Good implementations never materialise the dense vector.

5

What "modelling the joint" actually means

A generative model must assign a number to every (headline, label) pair — including the wrong pairings.

We want \(P(X,Y)\), and we choose to build it in the factored form

$$P(X,Y)\;=\;\underbrace{P(X\mid Y)}_{\text{P(headline}\mid\text{class)}}\times\underbrace{P(Y)}_{\text{P(class)}}.$$

Concretely, the clickbait headline needs two numbers, not one: the probability of that exact word pattern arising as a clickbait headline, and the probability of the same pattern arising as a normal headline. Classification will amount to asking which of the two is larger.

Headline \(x\)Score as clickbaitScore as normal
"You Won't Believe What This Celebrity Did Last Night!" \(P(x,\textit{Clickbait})=P(x\mid \textit{Clickbait})\,P(\textit{Clickbait})\) \(P(x,\textit{Normal})=P(x\mid \textit{Normal})\,P(\textit{Normal})\)
"Local Government Announces New Traffic Regulations Starting Monday" \(P(x,\textit{Clickbait})=P(x\mid \textit{Clickbait})\,P(\textit{Clickbait})\) \(P(x,\textit{Normal})=P(x\mid \textit{Normal})\,P(\textit{Normal})\)

Every headline is scored under both labels. The model is a full description of the world, not just of the boundary: it has an opinion about how probable a traffic-regulations headline is as the output of a clickbait writer. Classification compares the two numbers in each row; generation runs a row backwards.

A generative model can be run forwards

The factorisation is also a recipe for producing data. Flip a coin with bias \(p\) to choose a class; then draw a headline from that class's distribution over word patterns. This is why the model is called generative, and it is the interpretation the next few blocks lean on heavily — every parameter has a job in this recipe.

Counting the parameters

A distribution over \(2^d\) outcomes has \(2^d\) numbers but only \(2^d-1\) free ones, because they must sum to one — the last is determined. The same logic gives the class coin a single free parameter. So:

$$\underbrace{1}_{p\ \text{(coin bias)}}+\underbrace{(2^{d}-1)}_{P(X\mid \text{clickbait})}+\underbrace{(2^{d}-1)}_{P(X\mid \text{normal})}\;=\;2^{(d+1)}-1.$$

For \(d=2\) that is \(7\) parameters — fine. For \(d=50{,}000\) it is \(2^{50001}-1\), a number with roughly fifteen thousand digits. There are about \(10^{80}\) atoms in the observable universe.

Why this is fatal, precisely

It is not only memory. Each of the \(2^d\) cells is estimated by counting how many training headlines match that exact pattern. With \(n\) headlines and \(2^d\gg n\) cells, essentially every cell receives a count of zero, and the estimated probability of any headline you actually meet is zero. The model does not merely overfit; it memorises the training set exactly and assigns probability zero to everything else. This is the curse of dimensionality in its purest form.

Issue with modeling with full joint probability

We tried to model the full joint \(P(x,y)\) over \(d\) binary features and the label.

The object being fought over is the joint distribution of the \(d\) coordinates of \(x\) within a class, and it is precisely that joint structure which the next block throws away.

7

The naive Bayes assumption

One coin per word, flipped independently, once the class is known. The exponential becomes linear.

Instead of one giant table per class, can we imagine one coin per word, each with its own bias, deciding whether that word appears? And what do we pay for it?

Class-conditional independence. Given the label, the features are independent:

$$P(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_d\mid y)\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{d}P(x_i\mid y).$$

Two readings help. Written as a factorisation it is a licence to multiply. Written as a statement about information it says: once you know the headline is clickbait, learning that it contains "you" tells you nothing further about whether it contains "believe". All the dependence between words is assumed to run through the class label and nowhere else.

Sub-topic — independence versus conditional independence

Naive Bayes does not claim the words are independent. Marginally they are strongly dependent: "you" and "believe" co-occur far more often than chance across the whole corpus. The model explains that co-occurrence entirely by the hidden common cause — the class. Formally \(x_i\not\perp x_j\) but \(x_i\perp x_j\mid y\). Confusing the two is the single most common misreading of the assumption.

8

The generative story, in full

Two lines define the whole model. Everything afterwards is estimation and arithmetic.

How to generate one headline
  1. Choose the class. Flip the class coin: \(P(y=1)=p\), so \(P(y=0)=1-p\).
  2. Generate the words. For each vocabulary word \(i=1,\dots,d\), flip its coin for that class: word \(i\) is present with probability \(p_i^{\,y}\), absent with probability \(1-p_i^{\,y}\)
  3. Repeat to produce as much fake data as you like.

Written as a formula, for a headline \(x=[k_1,k_2,\dots,k_d]\) with each \(k_i\in\{0,1\}\):

$$P\big(x=[k_1,\dots,k_d]\ \big|\ y\big)\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{d}\big(p_i^{\,y}\big)^{k_i}\big(1-p_i^{\,y}\big)^{1-k_i}.$$

The exponents are a switch, not arithmetic in disguise. If word \(i\) is present, \(k_i=1\), the first factor is \(p_i^{\,y}\) and the second is \((1-p_i^{\,y})^0=1\). If absent, the roles swap. Every one of the \(d\) words contributes exactly one factor — including the 49,990 words that are absent, each contributing \(1-p_i^{\,y}\). That detail matters later: absence is evidence too.

And the joint, which is what the generative model is ultimately claiming to describe:

$$P(x,y)\;=\;P(y)\prod_{i=1}^{d}\big(p_i^{\,y}\big)^{k_i}\big(1-p_i^{\,y}\big)^{1-k_i},\qquad P(y)=p^{\,y}(1-p)^{1-y}.$$
A sanity check worth doing once

Does this define a valid distribution? Sum over all \(2^d\) patterns: the sum factorises into \(\prod_i\big[p_i^{\,y}+(1-p_i^{\,y})\big]=\prod_i 1 = 1\). Yes. And with \(d=2\) the four products in the slide's table — \((1-p_1)(1-p_2)\), \((1-p_1)p_2\), \(p_1(1-p_2)\), \(p_1p_2\) — are exactly the four terms of that expansion.

Which naive Bayes? Three standard variants

The naive assumption is about independence; it says nothing about what each \(P(x_i\mid y)\) looks like. Choosing that likelihood gives a different classifier for each kind of feature.

VariantFeature type\(P(x_i\mid y)\)Parameters per class
Bernoulli NB
this lecture		 binary — word present or absent \(\big(p_i^{y}\big)^{k_i}\big(1-p_i^{y}\big)^{1-k_i}\) \(d\) probabilities
Multinomial NB counts — how many times word \(i\) occurs \(\displaystyle\frac{(\sum_i c_i)!}{\prod_i c_i!}\prod_i \theta_{i}^{\,c_i}\) \(d\) probabilities summing to 1
Gaussian NB continuous — age, height, a sensor reading \(\mathcal{N}(x_i;\mu_i^{y},(\sigma_i^{y})^{2})\) \(d\) means and \(d\) variances
Categorical NB unordered categories with \(m\) levels a table of \(m\) probabilities per feature \(d(m-1)\)

The distinction between the first two is sharper than it looks. Bernoulli NB penalises every absent word explicitly, through the \((1-p_i^{y})\) factors; multinomial NB only ever touches words that actually occur. For short texts like headlines Bernoulli is usually competitive; for long documents multinomial is generally better, because absence of a rare word carries almost no information and Bernoulli spends a lot of arithmetic on it.

Gaussian NB:

For continuous features the counting estimates are replaced by the Gaussian MLEs, computed within each class:

$$\hat\mu_i^{\,y}=\frac{1}{n_y}\sum_{i:\,y_i=y} x_{i},\qquad \big(\hat\sigma_i^{\,y}\big)^{2}=\frac{1}{n_y}\sum_{i:\,y_i=y}\big(x_{i}-\hat\mu_i^{\,y}\big)^{2}.$$

The naive assumption here amounts to fitting each class with an axis-aligned Gaussian — a diagonal covariance matrix. Dropping the assumption and allowing full covariances gives quadratic discriminant analysis; sharing one full covariance across classes gives linear discriminant analysis. Naive Bayes, QDA and LDA are therefore the same generative idea at three levels of ambition.

10

What the assumption costs

The independence claim is false in every text corpus ever collected. The interesting question is why it so often does not matter — and when it does.

WHAT WE GAINED
  • \(2d+1\) parameters instead of \(2^{d+1}-1\).
  • Training is a single pass of counting — \(O(n\bar{d})\) for \(\bar d\) non-zero features per document.
  • Prediction is a sum of \(d\) logs, \(O(d)\) and trivially parallel.
  • Each parameter is estimated from many observations, so variance is low.
  • Works surprisingly well in practice, especially for text.
WHAT WE GAVE UP
  • The full joint could represent any pattern of co-occurrence; naive Bayes cannot represent co-occurrence at all beyond what the class explains.
  • "You won't believe this" is a stock phrase. Seeing you makes believe far more likely than the model allows.
  • Redundant evidence is double-counted, so the probabilities come out badly over-confident.
  • The boundary is constrained to be linear in the features (Part F).

Why it still works

The resolution is that classification needs much less than correct probabilities. To label a point correctly we only need the argmax over classes to be right — the ordering, not the values. Naive Bayes' estimates of \(P(y\mid x)\) are frequently wrong by orders of magnitude while landing on the correct side of \(0.5\). Domingos and Pazzani made this precise in 1997: the model's zero–one loss can be optimal over a far wider range of conditions than its probability estimates are accurate.

Practical consequence

Use naive Bayes' ranking, distrust its numbers. If you need probabilities you can act on — expected-cost decisions, thresholds tuned to a target precision — post-process them with Platt scaling or isotonic regression on a held-out set. Deduplicating near-identical features (stemming "movie"/"film" together, dropping one of a correlated pair) also helps directly.

The two formulas

Class prior — the proportion of training headlines labelled clickbait:

$$\hat p=\frac{k}{n},\qquad k=\sum_{i=1}^{n} I(y_i=1).$$

Word coins — the proportion of class-\(y\) headlines containing word \(j\):

$$\hat p_j^{\,y}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} I\big(k_j^{i}=1,\ y_i=y\big)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} I\big(y_i=y\big)},\qquad j\in\{1,2,\dots,d\},\ \ y\in\{0,1\}.$$

Read the second formula slowly, because the denominator is the part people get wrong. It is the number of headlines in class \(y\) — not \(n\), and not the number of headlines containing word \(j\). Each class is estimated in its own world; the two branches never share a denominator.

Notation: the indicator

\(I(\cdot)\) is \(1\) when its argument is true and \(0\) otherwise, so \(\sum_i I(\text{condition})\) simply counts. The numerator \(\sum_i I(k_j^i=1, y_i=y)\) counts headlines satisfying both conditions: labelled \(y\) and containing word \(j\).

Training, as an algorithm

All of this is one pass over the data with two counters per word per class. There is no optimisation loop, no learning rate, no convergence to check — which is why naive Bayes remains the standard baseline for text classification. Cost: \(O(n\bar d)\) time where \(\bar d\) is the average number of words per document, and \(O(2d)\) memory.

1. Computing the Class Prior

The class prior \(\hat{p}\) is the overall proportion of training headlines labelled as clickbait.

  • Total headlines (\(n\)): 5

Why counting is the maximum likelihood estimate

The slides assert the counting formulas. Three lines of calculus show they are forced.

The likelihood of the whole training set, using independence across documents (the i.i.d. assumption of block 1) and independence across words within a document (the naive assumption of block 7):

$$L=\prod_{i=1}^{n} P(y_i)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\big(p_j^{\,y_i}\big)^{k_j^{i}}\big(1-p_j^{\,y_i}\big)^{1-k_j^{i}}.$$

Take logs, which turns every product into a sum and every exponent into a multiplier:

$$\ell=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log P(y_i)\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big[k_j^{i}\log p_j^{\,y_i}+\big(1-k_j^{i}\big)\log\big(1-p_j^{\,y_i}\big)\Big].$$

The crucial structural fact: the parameters do not interact. The term containing \(p_j^{1}\) contains no other parameter, so the \(2d+1\) dimensional maximisation splits into \(2d+1\) independent one-dimensional problems. Collect the terms involving \(p_j^{1}\), writing \(a=\sum_i I(k_j^i=1,y_i=1)\) for the headlines that are clickbait and contain word \(j\), and \(n_1=\sum_i I(y_i=1)\) for all clickbait headlines:

$$\ell\big(p_j^{1}\big)=a\log p_j^{1}+(n_1-a)\log\big(1-p_j^{1}\big)+\text{const}.$$

This is exactly the Bernoulli log-likelihood. Differentiate and set to zero:

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial p_j^{1}}=\frac{a}{p_j^{1}}-\frac{n_1-a}{1-p_j^{1}}=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; a\big(1-p_j^{1}\big)=(n_1-a)\,p_j^{1} \;\Longrightarrow\; \hat p_j^{1}=\frac{a}{n_1}.$$

The second derivative, \(-a/(p_j^1)^2-(n_1-a)/(1-p_j^1)^2\), is negative throughout \((0,1)\), so this is a maximum. The same argument with \(y=0\) gives \(\hat p_j^{0}\), and applying it to the class coin gives \(\hat p = k/n\). The slides' formulas are not a convention — they are the unique stationary point of the likelihood.

Sub-topic — this is the estimation lecture, reused

\(\hat p = k/n\) for a Bernoulli is the standard result from the estimation lecture. What naive Bayes adds is the observation that its log-likelihood separates, so \(2d+1\) copies of that single result solve the whole problem at once. Separability is the real payoff of the independence assumption; the parameter count is just its visible symptom.

Prediction: running the story backwards
13

The decision rule

Training is over; a new headline arrives. Compare two numbers.

The pipeline so far: assume the generative process, estimate its \(2d+1\) parameters by MLE from the training data — that is the training. Now a test point \(x_{\text{test}}\) arrives and needs a label. Since the label is binary there are only two quantities to compare, \(P(y_{\text{test}}=1\mid x_{\text{test}})\) and \(P(y_{\text{test}}=0\mid x_{\text{test}})\), and we assign whichever is larger.

$$y_{\text{test}}=\begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } P(y_{\text{test}}=1\mid x_{\text{test}})\;\geq\;P(y_{\text{test}}=0\mid x_{\text{test}}),\quad\text{equivalently } \dfrac{P(y_{\text{test}}=1\mid x_{\text{test}})}{P(y_{\text{test}}=0\mid x_{\text{test}})}\geq 1,\\[1.1em] 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

Comparing two probabilities is the same as asking whether their ratio is at least \(1\) — a restatement that looks like pedantry and is not. Part F takes the log of that ratio, and the decision boundary falls out.

This rule has a name and a guarantee

Choosing the class with the highest posterior is the Bayes optimal decision rule (also the MAP rule). Among all possible classifiers it achieves the lowest possible error rate — provided the posterior it uses is the true one. Naive Bayes is therefore optimal in exactly one circumstance: when its independence assumption happens to hold and its parameters are exact. Everywhere else it is the right rule applied to a wrong posterior, which is the source of every failure in Part E.

If the two classes carry different costs — missing a fraudulent transaction hurts more than a false alarm — the threshold moves off \(1\) to the ratio of costs. Nothing else in the derivation changes.

14

Bayes' rule earns its keep

We modelled \(P(x\mid y)\) and \(P(y)\); the decision rule wants \(P(y\mid x)\). One line of algebra converts between them, and one cancellation makes it cheap.

$$P(y\mid x)=\frac{P(x\mid y)\,P(y)}{P(x)}.$$

Applied to both labels, this produces two expressions that share a denominator:

$$P(y_{\text{test}}=1\mid x_{\text{test}})=\frac{P(x_{\text{test}}\mid y_{\text{test}}=1)\times P(y_{\text{test}}=1)}{\textcolor{#B23A6B}{P(x_{\text{test}})}}$$ $$P(y_{\text{test}}=0\mid x_{\text{test}})=\frac{P(x_{\text{test}}\mid y_{\text{test}}=0)\times P(y_{\text{test}}=0)}{\textcolor{#B23A6B}{P(x_{\text{test}})}}$$

The term in rose is the same in both lines. \(P(x_{\text{test}})\) is the probability of seeing that headline at all, under either label — a nuisance, because computing it means summing over the classes anyway.

Since we only ever compare the two, the shared \(P(x_{\text{test}})\) cancels and can be ignored entirely. The comparison reduces to the numerators:

$$P(y=1\mid x)\ \gtrless\ P(y=0\mid x) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \underbrace{P(x\mid y=1)P(y=1)}_{\text{score for class }1}\ \gtrless\ \underbrace{P(x\mid y=0)P(y=0)}_{\text{score for class }0}.$$

And both numerators are things we estimated in Part C. Substituting the naive Bayes form:

$$\underbrace{P\big(x_{\text{test}}=[k_1,\dots,k_d]\ \big|\ y_{\text{test}}=1\big)}_{\textstyle\textcolor{#1F8A85}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\hat p_i^{\,1}\right)^{k_i}\left(1-\hat p_i^{\,1}\right)^{1-k_i}}} \;\times\;\underbrace{P\big(y_{\text{test}}=1\big)}_{\textstyle\textcolor{#B23A6B}{\hat p}}$$
LIKELIHOOD — \(d\) ESTIMATESOne factor per vocabulary word, each an estimate from block 11. Present words contribute \(\hat p_i^{\,1}\); absent words contribute \(1-\hat p_i^{\,1}\).
PRIOR — 1 ESTIMATEThe class coin \(\hat p=k/n\), applied once.

So \(d+1\) estimated numbers produce the class-1 score and the other \(d+1\) produce the class-0 score. In one line, for either label:

$$\text{score}(y)\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{d}\big(\hat p_i^{\,y}\big)^{k_i}\big(1-\hat p_i^{\,y}\big)^{1-k_i}\times \begin{cases}\hat p & y=1\\ 1-\hat p & y=0\end{cases}$$
$$\text{If}\quad\underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\hat p_i^{\,1}\right)^{k_i}\left(1-\hat p_i^{\,1}\right)^{1-k_i}\times\hat p}_{\text{score}(1)} \;\;\geq\;\; \underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\hat p_i^{\,0}\right)^{k_i}\left(1-\hat p_i^{\,0}\right)^{1-k_i}\times\left(1-\hat p\right)}_{\text{score}(0)}$$ $$\text{then assign } y_{\text{test}}=1,\qquad\text{else assign } y_{\text{test}}=0.$$

The complete classifier. Every symbol carries a hat: these are estimates from the training data, not the true probabilities — which is what makes the failure modes of Part E possible.

If you do want the probability, not just the label

Recover \(P(x_{\text{test}})\) by summing the two numerators — the law of total probability — since there is nothing else it can be:

$$P(y=1\mid x)=\frac{\text{score}(1)}{\text{score}(1)+\text{score}(0)}.$$

Useful when you need a ranking or a confidence, and free: the two scores are already computed. Bear block 10's warning in mind about how far to trust the resulting number.

15

Always work in log space

A detail the slides omit that decides whether any of this runs on a real computer.

The score is a product of \(d\approx 50{,}000\) numbers, each well below \(1\). Even a generous average factor of \(0.9\) gives \(0.9^{50000}\approx 10^{-2287}\). The smallest positive double-precision float is about \(10^{-308}\). Both class scores underflow to exactly \(0.0\), the comparison becomes \(0\geq 0\), and the classifier returns a constant label.

The fix is the one used everywhere in probabilistic modelling: compare logarithms. \(\log\) is strictly increasing, so it preserves the ordering and therefore the decision.

$$\log\text{score}(y)=\log P(y)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Big[k_i\log \hat p_i^{\,y}+(1-k_i)\log\big(1-\hat p_i^{\,y}\big)\Big].$$

Sums of a few tens of thousands of moderate negative numbers are perfectly representable. A second optimisation exploits sparsity — rewrite the sum so that only the words actually present need to be visited:

$$\log\text{score}(y)=\underbrace{\log P(y)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\big(1-\hat p_i^{\,y}\big)}_{\text{precompute once per class}}\;+\;\sum_{i:\,k_i=1}\log\frac{\hat p_i^{\,y}}{1-\hat p_i^{\,y}}.$$

The bracketed constant is computed once at training time. Classifying a ten-word headline then costs ten table lookups and ten additions, whatever \(d\) is.

And if you need the probability back

Do not exponentiate the log-scores directly — they are large negatives and \(e^{-2287}\) underflows again. Use the log-sum-exp trick: subtract the larger log-score \(M\) from both before exponentiating, which leaves the ratio unchanged and keeps every intermediate in range:

$$P(y=1\mid x)=\frac{e^{\,s_1-M}}{e^{\,s_1-M}+e^{\,s_0-M}},\qquad M=\max(s_1,s_0).$$
Part E
Failure modes and the fix
16

The zero-frequency problem

A single unseen word can veto an entire class, no matter what the other 49,999 say.

A test headline arrives containing a word that never appeared in any clickbait training headline. Its estimate is

$$\hat p_j^{\,1}=\frac{\sum_i I(k_j^i=1,\ y_i=1)}{\sum_i I(y_i=1)}=\frac{0}{n_1}=0.$$

The class-1 score is a product, and that word contributes the factor \((\hat p_j^1)^{k_j}=0^1=0\). The entire product collapses:

$$\text{score}(1)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\big(\hat p_i^{1}\big)^{k_i}\big(1-\hat p_i^{1}\big)^{1-k_i}\times\hat p\;=\;0.$$

The model now asserts that the headline is impossible as clickbait — not unlikely, impossible — and the label goes to class 0 whatever the evidence. A single rare word, appearing once, silences every other feature.

Why it is so common

This is not a rare edge case; it is guaranteed. Vocabularies follow Zipf's law: a large fraction of word types occur once or twice in any corpus, so most new documents contain at least one word never seen with some class. It is also an artefact of the estimator, not of the model. The true \(p_j^1\) is surely small, but it is not zero — nothing in the world forbids a clickbait writer from using that word. Maximum likelihood simply has no mechanism for distinguishing "never observed" from "impossible".

17

The full-frequency problem

The mirror image, and the one people forget. A word that appeared in every training headline of a class is just as destructive.

Suppose word \(j\) appears in all \(n_1\) clickbait training headlines. Then \(\hat p_j^{\,1}=n_1/n_1=1\), so \(1-\hat p_j^{\,1}=0\). Now consider a test headline that does not contain word \(j\), i.e. \(k_j=0\). Its contribution is

$$\big(\hat p_j^{1}\big)^{0}\big(1-\hat p_j^{1}\big)^{1-0}=1\times 0=0,$$

and the class-1 score is annihilated exactly as before. The model asserts that a clickbait headline must contain that word.

Which one bites in practice

Zero frequency, overwhelmingly — it takes only one unseen word, and rare words are everywhere. Full frequency needs a word present in all \(n_1\) documents of a class, which for large \(n_1\) happens mainly with stop-words, tiny training sets, or a leaked artefact (every scraped clickbait page carrying the word "sponsored", say). Note also that multinomial naive Bayes only suffers the zero-frequency half, because it never multiplies \((1-p)\) factors for absent words.

18

The fix: Laplace smoothing

Invent two headlines per class — one containing every word, one containing none — and add them to the data.

Both failures come from an estimate hitting \(0\) or \(1\). The remedy is to make those values unreachable. Add to each class two pseudo-documents:

  • a headline containing every word: \([1,1,1,\dots,1]\);
  • a headline containing no words: \([0,0,0,\dots,0]\).

Each class's denominator grows by \(2\); each word's numerator grows by \(1\) (from the all-ones document). The estimate becomes

$$\hat p_j^{\,y}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} I\big(k_j^{i}=1,\ y_i=y\big)+1}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} I\big(y_i=y\big)+2}\;=\;\frac{c_{j,y}+1}{n_y+2}.$$

With \(c_{j,y}=0\) this gives \(1/(n_y+2)\) — small, but strictly positive. With \(c_{j,y}=n_y\) it gives \((n_y+1)/(n_y+2)\) — close to one, but strictly below it. Every feature now has a non-zero chance of both appearing and not appearing, which is precisely what the two failure modes needed.

Deriving the boundary

Take the ratio form of the decision rule, take its logarithm, and watch a product of 50,000 factors turn into a weighted sum.

Start from the rule of block 13 — label 1 when the posterior ratio is at least one — and take logs of both sides, using \(\log 1=0\):

$$\log\frac{P(y_{\text{test}}=1\mid x_{\text{test}})}{P(y_{\text{test}}=0\mid x_{\text{test}})}\;\geq\;0.$$

Substitute Bayes' rule in both numerator and denominator. The \(P(x_{\text{test}})\) terms cancel, exactly as in block 14:

$$\log\frac{P(x_{\text{test}}\mid y=1)\,P(y=1)}{P(x_{\text{test}}\mid y=0)\,P(y=0)}\;\geq\;0.$$

Now insert the naive Bayes form and split the log of a product into a sum of logs:

$$\log\!\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\big(\hat p_i^{1}\big)^{k_i}\big(1-\hat p_i^{1}\big)^{1-k_i}}{\big(\hat p_i^{0}\big)^{k_i}\big(1-\hat p_i^{0}\big)^{1-k_i}}\times\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}\right)\geq 0$$ $$\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left[k_i\log\frac{\hat p_i^{1}}{\hat p_i^{0}}+(1-k_i)\log\frac{1-\hat p_i^{1}}{1-\hat p_i^{0}}\right]+\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}\;\geq\;0.$$

One more step. Expand \((1-k_i)\log\frac{1-\hat p_i^{1}}{1-\hat p_i^{0}}=\log\frac{1-\hat p_i^{1}}{1-\hat p_i^{0}}-k_i\log\frac{1-\hat p_i^{1}}{1-\hat p_i^{0}}\) and gather the \(k_i\) terms:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{d}k_i\log\!\left(\frac{\hat p_i^{1}\big(1-\hat p_i^{0}\big)}{\hat p_i^{0}\big(1-\hat p_i^{1}\big)}\right)\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\!\left(\frac{1-\hat p_i^{1}}{1-\hat p_i^{0}}\right)\;+\;\log\!\left(\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}\right)\;\geq\;0.$$

The decision function: naive Bayes is a linear classifier

Rename the two constant pieces and the punchline appears.

The first sum is linear in \(x_{\text{test}}=[k_1,k_2,\dots,k_d]\) — each \(k_i\) multiplied by a number that was fixed at training time. The remaining two terms do not involve \(x_{\text{test}}\) at all. So define

$$\underbrace{w_i=\log\frac{\hat p_i^{1}\big(1-\hat p_i^{0}\big)}{\hat p_i^{0}\big(1-\hat p_i^{1}\big)}}_{\text{one weight per word}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{b=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\frac{1-\hat p_i^{1}}{1-\hat p_i^{0}}\;+\;\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}}_{\text{a single bias}}$$
WEIGHT \(w_i\)The log odds ratio of word \(i\): how much more likely the word is under clickbait than under normal, measured on the odds scale.
BIAS \(b\)Two constants: the total absence penalty over the whole vocabulary, plus the log prior odds of the class.
$$\text{If } w^{\top}x_{\text{test}}+b\;\geq\;0 \ \Longrightarrow\ y_{\text{test}}=1,\qquad\text{else } y_{\text{test}}=0.$$

This is the perceptron rule from block 3, reached without ever intending to. Bernoulli naive Bayes has a linear decision boundary. All the probabilistic machinery — coins, tables, Bayes' rule, MLE — has produced a hyperplane, and the whole trained model can be stored as a weight vector and a scalar.

Sub-topic — this is a sigmoid, exactly

Keep the score rather than thresholding it. Let \(s=w^{\top}x+b=\log\frac{P(y=1\mid x)}{P(y=0\mid x)}\). Since the two posteriors sum to one,

$$P(y=1\mid x)=\frac{e^{s}}{1+e^{s}}=\sigma\big(w^{\top}x+b\big).$$

Bernoulli naive Bayes and logistic regression therefore share an identical functional form for the posterior. They differ only in how \(w\) and \(b\) are chosen: naive Bayes computes them in closed form from class-conditional counts, logistic regression optimises them to maximise the conditional likelihood \(\prod_i P(y_i\mid x_i)\) directly. Same hypothesis class, two different objectives — which is exactly what the next block measures.

When the boundary is not linear

Linearity is a consequence of the Bernoulli likelihood, not of naive Bayes as such. It survives for multinomial NB and for Gaussian NB with variances shared across classes. It fails for Gaussian NB with per-class variances, where the \(x^2\) terms no longer cancel and the boundary becomes a quadratic surface — an ellipse or a hyperbola.

Where the slide-29 error would hide

Here \(\hat p = 0.5\), so \(\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}=0\) and multiplying it by \(d=4\) changes nothing. This is exactly why the mistake survives a casual check. Rerun the example with a \(70/30\) class split and the erroneous bias is off by \(3\times\log\frac{0.7}{0.3}\approx 2.54\) — enough to flip this very prediction.

Attempt each before opening the answer. Several target the exact places where the slides are easy to misread.

A generative classifier uses \(d=12\) binary features and 3 classes, modelling each class-conditional as a full joint table. How many free parameters?

Class prior over 3 classes: \(3-1=2\) free. Each class-conditional is a distribution over \(2^{12}=4096\) patterns, so \(4095\) free each, \(3\times4095=12285\). Total \(12287\). Under the naive Bayes assumption it would instead be \(2+3\times12=38\). The general formulas: \((C-1)+C(2^{d}-1)\) versus \((C-1)+Cd\).

State the naive Bayes assumption, and give a concrete pair of words in the clickbait setting for which it is clearly false.

\(P(x_1,\dots,x_d\mid y)=\prod_i P(x_i\mid y)\): given the class label, the features are independent. "You" and "believe" break it — conditional on a headline being clickbait, seeing you raises the probability of believe far above its marginal rate within the class, because "you won't believe" is a fixed phrase. The model can only explain their co-occurrence through the class, and so under-explains it.

Why is a distribution over \(2^d\) patterns worth \(2^d-1\) parameters rather than \(2^d\)?

The probabilities must sum to \(1\), which is one linear constraint, so the last value is determined by the others. The same reasoning gives a coin one parameter rather than two, and a \(C\)-class prior \(C-1\) rather than \(C\). If you forget this you get \(2^{d+1}+1\) instead of \(2^{d+1}-1\) — the arithmetic barely changes but the reasoning is the thing being tested.

Training data: 4 of 10 headlines are clickbait. The word "shocking" appears in 3 of those 4 and in 0 of the 6 normal ones. Give the unsmoothed and Laplace-smoothed estimates, and explain what changes.

Unsmoothed: \(\hat p=4/10=0.4\); \(\hat p_{\text{shocking}}^{\,1}=3/4=0.75\); \(\hat p_{\text{shocking}}^{\,0}=0/6=0\). That last zero means any test headline containing "shocking" gets \(\text{score}(0)=0\) and is classified clickbait regardless of every other word — the zero-frequency problem.

Smoothed with \(\alpha=1\): \(\hat p_{\text{shocking}}^{\,1}=4/6\approx0.667\), \(\hat p_{\text{shocking}}^{\,0}=1/8=0.125\), and \(\hat p=5/12\approx0.417\). The word remains strong evidence — its weight is \(\log\frac{0.667\times0.875}{0.125\times0.333}\approx\log 14\approx 2.64\) — but it can now be outvoted.

A word appears in every one of the 200 clickbait training headlines. Which failure mode does this create, what triggers it, and which test headlines are affected?

Full frequency. \(\hat p_j^{\,1}=200/200=1\), so \(1-\hat p_j^{\,1}=0\). It is triggered by a test headline that does not contain the word: the factor \((1-\hat p_j^1)^{1-k_j}=0^1=0\) kills \(\text{score}(1)\), and every such headline is forced to class 0. Headlines that do contain the word are unaffected, since the exponent is then \(0\) and the factor is \(1\). Laplace smoothing gives \(201/202\) instead of \(1\) and the veto disappears.

Show that adding the two pseudo-documents of slide 26 is equivalent to MAP estimation with a \(\text{Beta}(2,2)\) prior.

Posterior for a Bernoulli parameter under \(\text{Beta}(\alpha_0,\beta_0)\) with \(c\) successes in \(n_y\) trials is \(\text{Beta}(\alpha_0+c,\ \beta_0+n_y-c)\), whose mode is \(\frac{\alpha_0+c-1}{\alpha_0+\beta_0+n_y-2}\). With \(\alpha_0=\beta_0=2\) this is \(\frac{c+1}{n_y+2}\) — the add-one formula exactly. The all-ones pseudo-document supplies the \(+1\) success and the all-zeros one the \(+1\) failure; together they are the two pseudo-observations that \(\text{Beta}(2,2)\) encodes.

Starting from \(\log\frac{P(y=1\mid x)}{P(y=0\mid x)}\geq0\), derive \(w\) and \(b\) for Bernoulli naive Bayes. How many times does the prior-odds term appear?

Cancel \(P(x)\), substitute the product form, split the log:

\(\sum_i\big[k_i\log\frac{\hat p_i^1}{\hat p_i^0}+(1-k_i)\log\frac{1-\hat p_i^1}{1-\hat p_i^0}\big]+\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}\geq0\).

Expanding the \((1-k_i)\) bracket and collecting \(k_i\) gives \(w_i=\log\frac{\hat p_i^{1}(1-\hat p_i^{0})}{\hat p_i^{0}(1-\hat p_i^{1})}\) and \(b=\sum_i\log\frac{1-\hat p_i^1}{1-\hat p_i^0}+\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}\).

Once. There is one class coin per headline, so the prior odds enters the ratio a single time. Slide 29's \(d\times\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}\) is wrong; it comes from leaving that term inside the sum on slide 28.

If naive Bayes and logistic regression produce boundaries of the same functional form, why do they ever disagree?

Same hypothesis class, different objective. Naive Bayes maximises the joint likelihood \(\prod_i P(x_i,y_i)\), which forces it to spend effort modelling \(P(x\mid y)\) correctly and constrains \(w\) to the closed-form log-odds-ratio values. Logistic regression maximises the conditional likelihood \(\prod_i P(y_i\mid x_i)\), choosing whatever \(w\) classifies best without regard to how \(x\) was generated. When the independence assumption is violated the joint-likelihood optimum is not the conditional-likelihood optimum, and the two land on different hyperplanes — logistic regression on the better one, given enough data.

A test headline contains 8 words; the vocabulary has 50,000. How many factors enter \(\text{score}(1)\), and how would you compute it without underflow?

All \(50{,}001\) of them: 8 factors \(\hat p_i^{1}\), \(49{,}992\) factors \((1-\hat p_i^{1})\), and the prior \(\hat p\). Absence is evidence in Bernoulli naive Bayes, which is precisely what distinguishes it from the multinomial variant.

Compute in logs. Precompute \(B_y=\log P(y)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log(1-\hat p_i^{y})\) once per class at training time, then for each test point add \(\sum_{i:k_i=1}\log\frac{\hat p_i^{y}}{1-\hat p_i^{y}}\) — eight lookups and eight additions.

You duplicate one informative feature ten times. What happens to naive Bayes' accuracy and to its reported probabilities? What would logistic regression do?

That feature's log-odds contribution is counted ten times, so the score is dominated by it and the reported posterior is driven towards \(0\) or \(1\) — severe over-confidence. Accuracy typically falls, because the duplicated evidence drowns out the genuinely independent features (see block 10's figure). Logistic regression fits the weights jointly: the ten copies share the weight that one would have received, and the decision is essentially unchanged. This is the cleanest single statement of what conditional independence buys and costs.

Under what circumstances is naive Bayes the Bayes-optimal classifier?

When the features really are conditionally independent given the class and the parameters are the true ones. Then \(P(y\mid x)\) computed by the model is the true posterior, and choosing its argmax is the Bayes rule, which no classifier can beat. In practice neither condition holds — the assumption is false and the parameters are estimates — so naive Bayes is the optimal rule applied to an imperfect posterior. Worth noting the converse too: exact conditional independence is sufficient but not necessary for correct labels, since only the argmax has to survive the approximation.

Sketch how you would extend everything above from 2 classes to \(C\) classes.

The model: a prior \(P(y=c)=\pi_c\) with \(\sum_c\pi_c=1\), and a set of \(d\) word coins per class. Parameters: \((C-1)+Cd\). Estimation: \(\hat\pi_c=n_c/n\) and \(\hat p_j^{\,c}=(c_{j,c}+\alpha)/(n_c+2\alpha)\). Prediction: compute the log score for every class and take the argmax — the pairwise ratio trick no longer reduces things to a single comparison, but the log-score computation is unchanged. The boundary between any pair of classes remains linear, so the overall partition is a set of convex polyhedral cells, exactly as in multiclass logistic regression.
